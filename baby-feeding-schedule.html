Baby Feeding Schedule by Age (0–12 Months)

There is no perfect feeding clock

New parents often want a schedule because it makes the day feel more manageable. Babies, however, do not always follow one. Hunger cues, growth, sleep, and medical needs all affect feeding frequency. Rooting, sucking movements, and hands moving toward the mouth can mean your baby is ready to feed. Turning away or slowing down can mean they are full.

Use the age ranges below as a guide, not as a strict timetable. If your baby was born early or has trouble gaining weight, follow the plan given by your healthcare professional.

Birth to one month

Breastfed newborns commonly feed eight to twelve times in twenty-four hours. Formula-fed babies also need frequent feeds, although amounts vary. Some newborns are so sleepy that they need waking for feeds until they are feeding well and have regained birth weight.

During this stage, diaper output and weight checks matter more than a beautifully organized feeding chart. Contact a clinician if your baby is difficult to wake, feeds poorly, or has noticeably fewer wet diapers.

One to five months

As babies grow, some become quicker at feeding and may go longer between feeds. Others continue to cluster feed, especially during growth spurts. That does not automatically mean milk supply is low. Whether you breastfeed, bottle-feed, or combine both, respond to your baby's hunger and fullness cues.

Milk remains the main food at this age. Watching adults eat does not mean a four-month-old needs solids. For most babies, complementary foods begin around six months.

Six to eight months

Keep breast milk or formula in the routine while introducing small meals of safe, nutrient-rich food. WHO guidance suggests complementary foods two to three times a day from six to eight months. Try iron-rich options, soft vegetables, and fruit. Adjust texture as your baby learns to eat.

Stay with your baby while eating and avoid choking hazards. Food exploration matters more than finishing a portion.

Nine to twelve months

Babies usually become more confident with soft finger foods and varied textures. Complementary meals often increase to three or four per day, with snacks as appropriate. Breast milk or formula still provides important nutrition through the first year.

Appetite may change from one day to the next. Watch growth and overall well-being rather than counting every spoonful. If you are worried about feeding, ask your pediatric clinician for an individualized plan.
