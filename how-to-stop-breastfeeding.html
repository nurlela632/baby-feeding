Baby Feeding Tips (Part 1) - How to Stop Breastfeeding?

There is no single right time

Some families wean because their child is ready; others make the change for work, health, or personal reasons. You can approach weaning at a pace that fits your circumstances. Breastfeeding can continue for two years or beyond if parent and child wish. If your baby is under twelve months, breast milk feeds that are dropped generally need replacing with appropriate infant formula rather than cow's milk as the main drink.

Make changes gradually

Dropping one feed at a time often gives your body time to adjust and your child time to get used to a different routine. You might begin with a feed that seems less important to your child and keep familiar comfort rituals, such as cuddling or reading. Sudden weaning can lead to uncomfortable breast fullness. If you need to stop quickly for a medical reason, ask your healthcare professional for individualized guidance.

Care for your body

As feeding frequency decreases, breasts may feel full or tender. Expressing a small amount for comfort may help, but removing large amounts can continue stimulating supply. Seek medical care for fever, worsening redness, or significant breast pain. Emotional reactions can also be mixed: relief, sadness, or both. It is reasonable to ask for support during the transition.

Focus on the child, not the calendar

Older babies and toddlers may ask to nurse for reassurance as much as hunger. Offer closeness and age-appropriate food or drinks while respecting your child's feelings. If your child is not growing well, has a restricted diet, or needs a specialized formula, plan weaning with a pediatric clinician. The goal is a safe, manageable change, not meeting someone else's deadline.

A practical reminder

Different babies respond to different approaches, so advice from another parent may not fit your situation. Focus on safe positioning, your baby's cues, and whether feeding is effective over time. If something feels painful, confusing, or consistently difficult, ask for individualized help. A brief feeding assessment can often clarify what is happening and what to try next.
